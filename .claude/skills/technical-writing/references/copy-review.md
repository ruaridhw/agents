# Copy review

## Overview

AI copy fails in a specific way: it performs the shape of good writing without doing the work. The shapes below are load-bearing when they carry information and dead weight when they don't. This is the pass that finds the dead ones.

**Core test for every flagged line: does it survive deletion?** If cutting it loses nothing a reader needed, it was decoration. Cut it.

## The Pass

1. **Draft without self-censoring.** Editing while drafting produces stiff copy.
2. **Re-read the finished draft against the kill list.** Not from memory — scan the list.
3. **For each hit: quote the exact line to yourself, name the tell, then cut or rewrite.** Quoting is the mechanism. You cannot fix a line you haven't isolated.
4. **Re-read the rewrites.** A fix commonly plants a fresh tell — most often a triplet or a kicker.
5. **Emit the fixed copy only.**

**Do not narrate the pass.** No "I've tightened this" preamble, no list of what you cut. Announcing the edit is itself a tell. Show quoted findings only when the user asked you to review copy, or when a cut removed something substantive they may want back.

## Kill List

Quote the line, then cut or rewrite.

1. **That's not X. That's Y.** Keep only Y.
2. **Two short bits stuck together.** "Fast. Simple." Pick one.
3. **Empty metaphor pair.** "Less a hammer, more a scalpel." Say the action.
4. **Clapping for itself.** "And that matters." / "That's the part everyone misses." Delete.
5. **X is the Excel of Y.** Name the job instead.
6. **Throat-clearing.** "Here's the thing." / "Let me be clear." Start later.
7. **Always three things.** Two or five is fine. Always-three is not.
8. **Fake ranges.** "5 to 10 minutes." A number you didn't measure means you never did it. Cut the number or go measure.
9. **Recap endings.** "In short..." / "At the end of the day..." Stop on the last real point.
10. **Participial tail.** "...shipped Tuesday, underscoring the team's commitment." / "...ensuring seamless delivery." The tail restates the clause it hangs off. Cut it, or promote it to a sentence with a real subject.
11. **Copula avoidance.** "serves as," "stands as," "represents," "boasts," "functions as." Use *is* and *has*.
12. **Borrowed authority.** "Industry experts say," "studies show," "many argue," "it's widely regarded." Name the source or drop the claim. Same family as #8: invented credibility.
13. **Hedge stack.** "It's worth noting that, generally speaking, in most cases..." Say it once, or don't say it.
14. **False balance.** "Critics argue X, supporters maintain Y — the truth is somewhere in between." Says nothing. Take the position or cut the paragraph.
15. **Pseudo-profound kicker.** "The real question isn't what it does — it's what we do with it." Written to be quoted, not read. Delete.
16. **Rhetorical question you then answer.** "So what does this mean? It means..." Delete the question, keep the answer.
17. **Orphaned "this."** "This matters because..." with no clear referent. Name the noun.
18. **Puffery vocabulary.** leverage, unlock, navigate, seamless, robust, ecosystem, landscape, journey, delve, tapestry, testament, pivotal, vibrant. See [the word list](copy-review-word-list.md).
19. **Em dash as a tic.** Fine occasionally. When it appears in most paragraphs, it's doing punchy-emphasis work a comma or a period should do.
20. **Cadence uniformity.** Every sentence 12–18 words, every bullet the same length, every section the same shape. Vary it, or cut what's only there for symmetry.
21. **Flattery and warmth garnish.** "Great question!" / "I'm glad you asked." / "Hope this helps!" Distinct from #6 — the fix isn't starting later, it's deleting the sentence.
22. **The product's word for the reader's thing.** "Every support email enters the pipeline and becomes a ticket" to a reader who calls it a complaint, a refund, an order that went wrong. Use the noun the reader uses in their own meetings. The product's internal noun (ticket, pipeline, workflow, module, workspace, instance) belongs only next to a screen that labels it that way, and never in a heading.
23. **Stamped template.** The same sentence shape on every page: "A home for every X", "Every X becomes a Y", "Meet your new Z". One instance was written; the rest were filled in. Write each from its own subject.

## Calibration — Don't Overcorrect

Scrubbing every pattern produces a different artificial voice. These are tells because they're **empty**, not because the shape is banned.

- A contrast that carries real information stays. "It's not a rewrite, it's a config change" is a fact.
- Three things is right when there are exactly three things.
- Em dashes are good punctuation.
- Keep any number you actually measured, and keep the range if the range is real.
- Never apply this to: quoted text, the user's own writing unless they asked you to edit it, code, identifiers, or established domain and API terms.
- If a rewrite is longer or vaguer than the original, keep the original.

## Red Flags — Run the Pass Again

- You wrote a closing paragraph and it doesn't add a fact.
- You reached for a third item to round out a pair.
- You wrote a number you didn't get from a tool, a file, or the user.
- You used *serves as*, *underscores*, or *highlights*.
- A sentence sounds strong and you can't say what it claims.
- You're describing something as "not just" anything.
- A person in the reader's job would not say the sentence out loud about their own work.

## Rationalizations

| Excuse | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| "It's only a Slack message" | Short copy is where tells are most visible. Run the pass. |
| "The rhythm needs the third item" | Rhythm is what the third item is for. That's the tell. |
| "The range is roughly right" | Roughly right is invented. Cut it or measure it. |
| "The reader needs a summary" | If they need one, the body failed. Fix the body. |
| "It reads flat without the kicker" | Flat and true beats resonant and empty. |
| "I'll just soften it with a hedge" | Hedges hide that you don't know. Say what you know. |
